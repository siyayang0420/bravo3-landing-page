Jacob Ung has been writing about restaurants since 2012, first under the name Premeditated Gluttony and now as Just Savour Life. He is chief operating officer of the Chinese Restaurant Awards, he has taken WSET courses in wine, spirits and sake, and he is explicit on his own site about how he works: “No meals traded for reviews, in either direction. No paid reviews.”

He is also explicit about what he is looking for. Food, he writes, is mostly the excuse: “the people at the table are more interesting than what’s on it.”

He describes himself as “Chinese by ancestry, born in the Philippines, raised in Canada — and not done with any of them,” which is a sentence about arriving somewhere and keeping what you brought. It turns out to be a useful thing to have at this particular table.

Because Kavita is a person.

![Max So and Jacob Ung laughing at a banquette table at Kavita, plates of curry and flatbread in front of them and a camera at the edge of the frame](images/p1.webp)

Max So, Bravo’s chief executive, took him there for the first episode of this series. The restaurant sits on West 3rd Avenue in Olympic Village: forty-six seats, an open kitchen, and a little under a year of trading behind it. It opened in October 2025 and by the following summer had been placed ninety-sixth on Canada’s 100 Best Restaurants and given Vancouver Magazine’s gold for Best Indian.

The chef and owner is Tushar Tondvalkar. He is from Mumbai, and he cooked at Gaggan and at Gaa in Bangkok, both Michelin-starred, before coming to Vancouver and working through Blue Water Café, Bauhaus and The Fish House in Stanley Park. He ran the kitchen at Mumbai Local and built a retail line called The Indian Pantry. Kavita is the first room that is his.

He named it after his late mother. *Kavita* means poetry; he glosses it more loosely as script, or the telling of a story. What he told VITA Daily a week before this dinner explains the choice about as economically as it can be explained: “I grew up in the kitchen with my mother and grandmother. They inspired my love of cooking.”

![Guests seated along the counter at Kavita while a cook in an apron works in front of them, pendant lights and curved bottle shelves behind](images/p2.webp)

So the name is a person and the food is regional rather than national, drawn from particular communities in particular parts of India, which is a harder thing to put on a menu than “Indian” and a more honest one. There is no butter chicken.

What makes the menu coherent is a theory, and Tondvalkar laid it out over the kebabs. The technique came into India from outside: Persian and Iranian invasions brought the skewer and the fire. What happened next was Indian. The spices were local, the hands were local, and the dishes that came out the other side belong to India rather than to the people who brought the method.

That is a working answer to the authenticity question, and it does two jobs at once. It explains the traditional dishes, since a kebab is not less Indian for having arrived, and it licenses the untraditional ones. The lamb chops go into the tandoor under Bengali five-spice and garam masala, which is orthodox enough. The naan comes with chilli, cheese and garlic in it, which is traditional nowhere at all and on the menu anyway. By his own logic that is not a compromise. It is the same process, still running.

![The table at Kavita mid-meal — a basket of flatbread, a chutney-topped naan, the plate of lamb chops and a small bowl of pickled onion, with hands reaching across](images/p3.webp)

The sourcing follows the same instinct pointed at British Columbia rather than at history. Produce comes from local farms and the meat from local butchers, which for a cuisine built on spice and slow cooking is less obvious than it sounds — the easy version of this food travels frozen and arrives anywhere.

The open kitchen is worth noticing on the way past. Most kitchens are behind a door, for good reasons: heat, noise, the fact that a busy service is not a performance. Kavita put the work where the room can see it, which is a decision to be watched.

![Tushar Tondvalkar standing at the table in conversation with Max So and Jacob Ung, a camera tripod at the edge of the frame](images/p4.webp)

Asked how a year-old restaurant ends up on a national list, Tondvalkar did not talk about technique. He credited the kitchen and the front-of-house team. His general manager, Yash Shah, has been his friend since kindergarten.

![Charred eggplant under piped white purée with wild rice and nasturtium leaves, in a wide white bowl](images/p5.webp)

Which brings it back to where Jacob started. The pastry, the spice route, the ninety-sixth place — all of it is downstream of a man who learned to cook standing next to his mother, and who has put her name on the outside of the building where fifty people a night can watch him work.
